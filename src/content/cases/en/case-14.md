Confidence is not evidence. Today we interrogate a claim the way a fact-checker would. The same five questions work on any claim: a chatbot answer, a social post, an article, a person.

**Age rules come first.** Chatbots have age rules, and they differ. OpenAI's help page currently says ChatGPT is not meant for children under 13, and that children aged 13 to 18 need parental consent. Others differ. If you cannot use a chatbot, interrogate a social post or an article instead. The exercise works either way.

## The five questions

1. **Where did it come from?** Did it give a source?
2. **Does the source exist, and does it really say that?** Open it. Do not take the chatbot's word for it.
3. **Lateral reading.** Leave the page. What do two independent sources say?
4. **Ask again in different words.** Does the answer stay the same?
5. **Push.** Ask "Are you sure?" Does it hold, or flip? If it flips, that tells you about the tool, not about the truth.

## Your mission

1. Pick a harmless topic where you already know part of the answer. No health, legal or safety questions.
2. Read the chatbot's terms and privacy policy, and note the date.
3. Never type personal details, school names or other people's information into it.
4. Run all five questions on each claim and fill in one row per claim in the log.
5. Give each claim a verdict: supported, not supported, mixed, or can't tell. Not supported and can't tell are different. Can't tell means you do not know, and you say so.
6. Pick 5 claims, and find one verdict you changed your mind about.

## Lateral reading

Researchers at Stanford found that professional fact-checkers judged online sources more accurately and faster than historians and students. The reason: they left the page and checked what other sources said about it, instead of staying on the page and judging it from the inside.

<div class="twist">

**The trap: asking it to check itself.** In 2023, in a case called Mata v. Avianca in a New York federal court, two lawyers filed a brief citing court cases that did not exist. A chatbot had made them up. The judge fined them $5,000. One of the lawyers later said he had asked the chatbot whether the cases were real, and it said they were. Asking the tool to confirm itself is not checking. It is asking the same witness twice.

</div>

## Keep the claim the right size

One chatbot and a handful of questions is an anecdote, not a rating. Report what happened, not what you expected. If every source checks out, say so. An honest "this one was good" makes your case stronger.
